# Personal Site Coding Brief

## Working concept

**A guided, playable developer studio**

Build a bold, immersive personal portfolio that feels like entering a small digital world created by a technically minded designer/developer. The experience should combine a memorable 3D centerpiece, calm and legible interface design, developer-native language, purposeful motion, optional sound, and clear routes to projects, background, and contact information. Visitors should be able to follow a short authored journey or explore freely.

The goal is to attract clients. Visitors should leave with three impressions:

1. This person has strong creative-development skills.
2. This person has a distinct point of view and personality.
3. It is still easy to understand their work and contact them.

## Selected references

### 1. Wisnu Wicaksono

Reference: [wiscaksono.com](https://wiscaksono.com/)

What stands out:

- A unique, nerdy way of speaking in developer language.
- The interface resembles a piece of software or a terminal window rather than a standard marketing website.
- Monospace typography, ASCII-style graphics, compact labels, window controls, and command-like navigation create a coherent identity.
- The visual system is restrained, which lets the personality come from the language and interface conventions.

What to borrow:

- A developer-native copy voice and interface vocabulary.
- Small details such as status labels, command prompts, file-like section names, comments, coordinates, version labels, or loading messages.
- The feeling that visitors are operating a personal tool or environment.

Do not copy the terminal surface literally. The important principle is that the visual language and writing should feel native to the person building the site.

### 2. Bruno Simon

Reference: [bruno-simon.com](https://bruno-simon.com/)

Selected qualities: **3D and interaction**

What stands out:

- Seamless 3D interaction is the experience itself, not a decorative background.
- A simple, immediately understandable input becomes the navigation model.
- The environment rewards exploration and demonstrates technical skill before the visitor reads a biography.
- The opening screen clearly teaches the interaction and offers sound control.

What to borrow:

- One strong spatial interaction model that visitors can understand in seconds.
- The idea that navigating the portfolio should itself demonstrate creative-development ability.
- A short, visual onboarding prompt rather than a long explanation.
- Immediate feedback for every interaction.

Do not reproduce the car, driving mechanic, environment, or composition. Create a spatial metaphor connected to this portfolio owner's own practice.

### 3. Robin Payot

Reference: [robinpayot.com](http://www.robinpayot.com/)

Selected qualities: **typography, navigation, project stories, layout, motion, 3D, interaction, and color**

What stands out:

- The overall combination feels calming and interactive instead of visually exhausting.
- The entrance is minimal and confident, creating anticipation before revealing the richer experience.
- 3D navigation, motion, sound effects, layout, typography, and color behave as one system.
- The work is not presented as disconnected cards. Navigation and storytelling are part of the creative concept.

What to borrow:

- A calm outer shell around the immersive elements.
- Sound as a subtle layer of feedback, with obvious controls.
- Transitions that make movement between sections feel spatial and continuous.
- Project stories that feel connected to the environment rather than bolted on afterward.
- Controlled typography and generous space to balance the technical spectacle.

Do not add effects merely to match the reference. Every visual, audio, and motion decision should reinforce the same world and interaction model.

### 4. Aristide Benoist

Reference: [aristidebenoist.com](https://aristidebenoist.com/)

Selected qualities: **typography, layout, motion, and navigation**

What stands out:

- A highly controlled, cinematic layout with very few interface elements.
- Condensed display typography, small utility labels, and strong spatial composition establish authority.
- Navigation remains visible and understandable even when the central presentation is experimental.
- Motion and image treatment create intrigue without requiring a busy color palette.

What to borrow:

- Editorial restraint around the 3D experience.
- A strong display typeface paired with small, precise interface text.
- Persistent orientation cues: current section, next action, project position, and a clear route back.
- Cinematic transitions and intentional image framing.

Do not make usability mysterious. Preserve the atmosphere while keeping every important action discoverable.

### 5. Why One University

Reference: [why.one.university](https://why.one.university/)

Role in this direction: **guided discovery and narrative structure**

This should be treated as a secondary structural reference rather than a fifth competing visual style. Its useful contribution is the idea that an unusual experience can still lead visitors through a deliberate sequence, like moving through a compact course, chapter system, or guided curriculum.

What to borrow:

- A clear sense of progression through the experience.
- Information revealed in intentional stages rather than displayed all at once.
- A guided route for first-time visitors alongside optional free exploration.
- Chapter, module, lesson, milestone, or curriculum language where it fits naturally.
- A feeling that the visitor is learning how the creator thinks, not merely viewing finished work.

Potential application:

- The first visit can offer two routes: **Start the guided tour** or **Explore freely**.
- The guided route should move through identity, selected work, process, experiments, and contact in a short sequence.
- Projects can feel like modules in a personal curriculum, with each one demonstrating a different capability or lesson learned.
- Progress indicators can show where visitors are without turning the site into a literal school interface.

Do not copy university branding or force an educational metaphor over every page. Use the reference to improve pacing, progression, and comprehension. The public URL is currently unavailable, so exact visual details should not be copied or inferred without screenshots of the intended version.

## Conclusion drawn from the references

The intended site should not be a conventional scrolling portfolio and should not be a pure game. It should sit between an interactive digital world, a developer tool, a guided learning journey, and a refined editorial portfolio.

The central tension is important:

- **Immersive but calm**
- **Experimental but navigable**
- **Nerdy but approachable**
- **Technically impressive but still client-focused**
- **Playful on the surface, rigorous underneath**

The 3D environment should supply the memorable idea. Typography, layout, and navigation should supply clarity. Developer-native writing should supply personality. Sound and motion should supply feedback and atmosphere.

Why One University does not change the core aesthetic. It adds an authored path through the experience. The other four references determine how the site looks and feels; Why One helps determine how a first-time visitor moves through it.

## Experience direction

### Entry

- Show a meaningful visual quickly. Avoid a long cinematic loader.
- Introduce the spatial metaphor with one short sentence and one obvious action.
- Offer **Start the guided tour** and **Explore freely** without making either route feel secondary.
- Provide an always-visible sound toggle before any sound is played.
- Include a direct “View projects” route for visitors who do not want to explore the 3D experience.

### Primary environment

Create one compact, explorable 3D scene based on a metaphor that belongs to the portfolio owner. Possible directions include a creative workstation, miniature studio, navigable project constellation, virtual workshop, or a system of connected tools and artifacts.

The metaphor must provide a reason for each navigation destination to exist. Projects, about information, experiments, writing, and contact should feel like parts of the same place.

Do not build a large empty world. Prefer a small, art-directed scene with a few meaningful interactive objects.

The guided mode should use the same environment and content as free exploration. It is a recommended sequence through the world, not a second website or a separate tutorial.

### Navigation

- Use direct manipulation in the scene, but retain a persistent 2D navigation layer.
- Main destinations: **Work, About, Experiments or Writing, Contact**.
- Always communicate the current location and the available next actions.
- In guided mode, show progress and provide next, previous, exit tour, and skip-to-project controls.
- Provide a clear home/reset action.
- Make project names accessible without requiring precision pointer movement.
- Support keyboard navigation and conventional links alongside spatial controls.

### Project presentation

- Selecting a project should transition from the spatial environment into a readable case-study layer.
- Case studies should use normal document scrolling, semantic HTML, and a calm editorial layout.
- Each case study should cover context, role, problem, process, key decisions, implementation, and outcome.
- Retain a visual connection to the main world through color, motion, ambient details, or a miniature scene element.
- Provide obvious previous project, next project, close, and contact actions.

### About and personality

- Use developer-native writing without turning every sentence into a programming joke.
- Small labels can resemble commands, comments, paths, or system messages.
- Main paragraphs should remain warm, direct, and readable.
- Personality should come from specific details about interests, process, taste, and working style.

Example tone:

> I build interfaces where design systems, motion, and code meet. I care about the small feedback loops that make software feel alive.

Avoid generic phrases such as “passionate developer,” “turning ideas into reality,” and unexplained technology lists.

### Contact

- Make contact available from every major state.
- Use one strong invitation connected to the client goal, such as “Have an unusual interface in mind?”
- Include email, professional profile links, location or timezone, and availability.
- Do not hide contact information inside the 3D scene only.

## Visual system

### Palette

- Begin with a dark charcoal or near-black base inspired by the software-like and cinematic references.
- Choose one vivid accent family for the 3D world and active states.
- Allow warm pools of color or light inside the scene while keeping the 2D interface mostly neutral.
- Use color to indicate interaction, selection, location, and project identity.
- Avoid an uncontrolled rainbow palette.

### Typography

Use three clearly defined roles:

1. A distinctive condensed or characterful display face for names, project titles, and major transitions.
2. A precise monospace face for interface labels, coordinates, status messages, commands, and metadata.
3. A highly readable sans-serif face for case studies and longer copy.

Typography should remain part of the experience even when WebGL is unavailable.

### Composition

- Keep permanent interface chrome sparse.
- Use generous negative space and deliberate asymmetry.
- Treat small utility text as part of the art direction, not filler.
- Keep body text, controls, and navigation in the DOM rather than rendering them into the 3D canvas.

## Motion and sound

- Motion must communicate state, hierarchy, or spatial continuity.
- Use responsive hover, focus, press, selection, and loading feedback.
- Prefer a few authored transitions over constant ambient movement.
- Maintain consistent easing and timing across the 2D and 3D layers.
- Use subtle interface sounds for hover, selection, movement, or entering a project.
- Sound must be opt-in or begin only after a user gesture, have a persistent mute control, and remember the visitor's choice.
- Respect reduced-motion preferences and provide simpler crossfades or immediate state changes.

Avoid scroll hijacking, unnecessary cursor replacement, long unskippable transitions, and motion that prevents visitors from reaching information quickly.

## Responsive and fallback behavior

- Desktop receives the richest 3D experience.
- Mobile should use the same identity and content but simplify camera movement, scene complexity, and interaction.
- Touch targets must not depend on hover.
- Provide a non-WebGL fallback with a still or lightweight animated scene and the complete 2D navigation.
- The site must remain useful with sound muted, reduced motion enabled, or 3D disabled.

## Performance and accessibility requirements

- Render the shell, identity, navigation, and primary call to action before the full 3D scene is ready.
- Lazy-load heavy models, textures, audio, and secondary environments.
- Compress and reuse geometry, textures, and audio assets.
- Display useful progress rather than a decorative percentage that blocks the whole site.
- Keep project content indexable and selectable in semantic HTML.
- Provide keyboard access, visible focus states, descriptive labels, sufficient contrast, and alternatives for canvas-only information.
- Test with mouse, keyboard, touch, reduced motion, muted audio, slow networks, and WebGL unavailable.

## Suggested implementation phases

### Phase 1: Content and interaction model

- Finalize the personal spatial metaphor.
- Define the four main destinations and project list.
- Write the first draft of project, about, and contact copy.
- Prototype the complete journey with simple geometry and unstyled DOM panels.

### Phase 2: Visual shell

- Establish typography, palette, layout, persistent navigation, and interface language.
- Build accessible project and content panels.
- Verify mobile and non-WebGL routes before adding visual complexity.

### Phase 3: 3D centerpiece

- Create the compact environment and primary interaction.
- Connect scene objects to real routes and content.
- Optimize models, textures, lighting, and loading behavior continuously.

### Phase 4: Motion and sound

- Author transitions between the environment and content layers.
- Add restrained feedback sounds and persistent controls.
- Implement reduced-motion alternatives.

### Phase 5: Polish and validation

- Test discovery: can a new visitor find projects, understand the role, and reach contact without instructions?
- Test performance on average mobile and laptop hardware.
- Test every fallback and accessibility path.
- Remove effects that do not improve atmosphere, feedback, storytelling, or comprehension.

## Acceptance criteria

The first version is successful when:

- A visitor can describe the site's spatial metaphor after one interaction.
- The primary interaction is understandable within a few seconds.
- A first-time visitor can complete the guided route without guessing the next action, while a returning visitor can bypass it entirely.
- At least three projects are reachable through both the creative interface and conventional navigation.
- Every project remains readable without interacting with the 3D scene.
- Contact is reachable from every major state.
- Sound is controlled by the visitor and never traps or surprises them.
- Mobile, reduced-motion, keyboard, and non-WebGL experiences are intentionally designed.
- The result feels inspired by the four references but is not visually or mechanically identifiable as a clone of any one of them.

## Final instruction to the coding agent

Treat this as one coherent product, not a list of effects. Start by choosing the personal metaphor and mapping real content into it. Build a fast, accessible 2D shell first, then make the 3D layer progressively enhance that shell. Add a concise guided sequence through the same content rather than building a separate tour experience. At every decision, preserve the intended balance: immersive but calm, experimental but clear, authored but not restrictive, and technically expressive without hiding the work.
